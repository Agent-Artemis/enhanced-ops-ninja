The one thing to get right

Speed is a feature. What it lets them do is the value.

“We get you the data fast” is a thing we do, not a reason to buy. Every line below runs the same three steps, and so should you on a call.

1 · What they cannot see

Not a missing report — a number that does not exist anywhere until somebody builds it by hand.

2 · The decision it unlocks

A specific thing they do differently, with a timeframe. This week, not at year end.

3 · What that is worth

The mechanism, on their own numbers. Not our percentage — their arithmetic, done in their head.

The test: if a value line ends at “you see it sooner”, it is not finished. It has to end in something they would do, or something it would cost them not to.

Objections · every segment

The seven you will hear most

These come up whatever the industry. The answers are short on purpose — you are meant to be able to say them, not read them out.

I already have a dashboard.

Does everyone in the room trust the same number? Most dashboards get quietly checked against somebody's own spreadsheet before anyone acts on them. A dashboard nobody argues with is a different product from a dashboard nobody believes.

My controller pulls the numbers.

She does, and she is good at it — that is the problem. How many days a month is she spending on it, and what would you rather she did with them? A person doing this well is the most expensive way to do it, and it stops when she is away.

My data is a mess.

That is the job, not a prerequisite. You do not tidy the house before the cleaner arrives. Nobody comes in and asks you to clean it up first — sorting it out is the first two weeks of the work.

We are too small for this.

If you run more than one location and more than two systems that do not talk to each other, you are exactly the size. It is not about revenue — it is about how many places a number can hide.

How long does it take?

Two to three weeks to the first live number. Not a discovery phase, not a pilot — a number on a screen you can use.

What if we stop?

Six months to start, then month to month. Your data is yours and never leaves your own accounts — we join as an invited guest and you can remove us. The system that reads it is ours and we run it, so if it ends we switch it off: you keep every system and every number you had before, and what you lose is the screens.

It is expensive.

Against what? Turn it into a comparison, never a defence. Depending on how many systems and locations we connect it runs $4,500 to $7,000 a month, which is the cost of a low to mid-level employee. If you needed this information and someone had to pull it together by hand, would you hesitate to hire someone to complete that work? Think of this as a digital employee. It does not take PTO, does not need managing, does not leave after eighteen months, and it reads six systems before the 7am huddle. Then ask what the last decision made on a wrong number cost. They will have one.

Segment 01

Commercial & field services

The trades that run on trucks

Systems: ServiceTitan, Jobber or Housecall Pro · QuickBooks · payroll. Numbers: first-time fix rate, technician utilization, revenue per truck, callback rate.

What they cannot see today

Dispatch is in one system, invoicing in another, labour in a third. What a truck actually earned does not exist as a number until somebody builds a spreadsheet, and by then the month is closed.

Callbacks are worse: a return visit is a job they were paid for once and did twice, and most operators have no rate for it at all.

What fast data lets them do

Ride along with that tech this week, when his callback rate doubles — instead of finding out at year end that he cost them three customers.

Reroute tomorrow morning when unassigned calls cluster in one postcode, because they can see it is a routing problem and not a capacity one.

What that is worth

One truck running six per cent below the others, across a year. On five trucks, work out what the fifth one is costing.

Then take the callback rate, halve it, and multiply by a year of jobs. Every one of those is a visit paid for once and performed twice.

HVACLives on maintenance-agreement attrition and callback rate. The agreement base is the business, and churn in it shows up a year after the customer decided.
RoofingLives on crew-day margin and material waste. The crew-day is the unit of production, and waste and rework eat the job before the invoice is written.
PlumbingLives on ticket size and first-time fix. High volume, small jobs — a second visit destroys the economics of the first.
ElectricalLives on job costing against bid. Fixed price work where labour hours drift quietly and nobody costs the job until it closes.
Who in your network is this
  • Runs three or more service trucks, or has just added a second crew.
  • Bought out a competitor's route or a second branch in the last couple of years.
  • Still opens QuickBooks himself on a Sunday to work out how the month went.
  • Has said some version of “I don't know which of my guys actually makes me money”.
If they push back

ServiceTitan already gives me dashboards.

ServiceTitan reports on ServiceTitan. It does not know what QuickBooks says. The gap between them is where your margin hides — the completed job it counts and the invoice that never got raised are the same work, in two systems, with two different answers.

My techs' numbers are in the field, not in a system.

Then that is the first thing worth fixing, and it is days of work rather than quarters. If drive time is not recorded you cannot tell a capacity problem from a routing one — and those have completely different answers.

We are only one branch.

Then it is truck by truck instead of branch by branch. Same question, smaller unit. One branch with five trucks has the same problem as five branches — it is just closer to home.

Segment 02

Distribution & wholesale

Where the cash sits on a shelf

Systems: NetSuite, Acumatica or SAP Business One · a WMS · Fishbowl. Numbers: fill rate, on-time delivery, inventory turns, days on hand, backorder rate.

What they cannot see today

The same order looks different in the ERP, the warehouse system and the carrier portal. Inventory value is the number nobody will commit to out loud.

Fill rate gets quoted from whichever system gives the kindest answer, and nobody says which definition they used.

What fast data lets them do

Catch a supplier slipping on lead time in week two and move the reorder point before it becomes a backorder and a lost customer.

Decide which SKU class to stop carrying while the cash is still on the shelf, rather than after a stock count confirms it a quarter later.

What that is worth

Days on hand is cash. Take a week off days-on-hand across their inventory position and that is the number — they will know their own inventory value to the nearest hundred thousand.

Then ask what one backordered line costs them in reorders they never see again.

Who in your network is this
  • Holds stock in more than one location, or has just opened a second warehouse.
  • Implemented an ERP some years ago and half-configured it — everyone knows which bits nobody uses.
  • Has said “we don't really trust the inventory number” and then moved on as though that were normal.
If they push back

The ERP is our system of record.

For accounting, yes. It is not the system of record for whether the truck left on time — and the carrier portal does not tell it. The gap between them is where your service level actually lives.

We count stock twice a year.

So twice a year you know. The other three hundred and sixty-three days you are guessing, and every purchasing decision in between is made on the guess.

Our fill rate is fine.

Measured how? Line fill, order fill and case fill give three different answers off the same week — and the flattering one is usually the one being quoted. It is worth knowing which one you have been reporting.

Segment 03

Manufacturing

Where the shift that caused it has gone home

Systems: ERP · MES · shop-floor and machine data · quality. Numbers: OEE, machine utilization, downtime, scrap rate, throughput.

What they cannot see today

Shop-floor reality and the ERP rarely agree, and a variance is costed long after the shift that caused it has gone home.

Downtime gets written on paper at the end of a shift, from memory. That is a record, not a measurement.

What fast data lets them do

See a line drifting on scrap during the shift, while there is still a next run to fix — not in next month's variance report when the material is already in a skip.

Decide whether Thursday's second shift is worth running, on actual throughput rather than on the plan.

What that is worth

A single point of scrap on a line running all year. They know their material cost per unit; they can do that one in their head.

Then an hour of unplanned downtime per shift, costed at their own contribution margin rather than at a rate we invented.

Who in your network is this
  • Runs more than one line or cell, or more than one shift.
  • Has an MES that nobody fully trusts, or bought one and never finished rolling it out.
  • Has a plant manager who keeps his own whiteboard because the ERP number arrives too late to be useful.
If they push back

The MES already shows OEE.

On the machines it is connected to, on its own definition of availability. It does not know what the ERP costed the run at. Two systems, two OEEs, and neither of them is wrong — which is exactly the problem.

Our operators log all of this.

On paper, at the end of the shift, from memory. That is a record, not a measurement — and nobody is going to act on Tuesday's downtime when they read it on Friday.

We are not that automated.

Then the shop-floor data is the gap, and closing it is a much smaller job than an MES rollout. You do not need a connected factory to know which cell is losing you money.

Segment 04

Restaurants & food service

Where the P&L arrives too late to act on

Systems: Toast or Square · Restaurant365 · 7shifts. Numbers: food cost %, labor %, sales per labor hour, comp sales by location.

What they cannot see today

The two numbers that decide the week — food cost and labour — live in different systems from the sales that justify them, and each location reports on a slightly different definition.

Comp sales between sites get argued about rather than settled.

What fast data lets them do

Catch a site running two points over on labour on a Wednesday and fix the weekend schedule — instead of reading it in a P&L three weeks after the month closed, when the money has gone.

Move a manager who is getting it right to the site that is not, on evidence rather than on instinct.

What that is worth

One point of food cost across a group's annual revenue. They know their revenue; that arithmetic takes them a second and it lands harder than any percentage we could quote.

Then the same again on labour: bring the worst site to the best site's number and hold it for a year.

Who in your network is this
  • Owns more than one location, or is a franchisee with several units.
  • Has said the P&L arrives too late to do anything with — usually with a shrug, as though that were just how it is.
  • Is opening a new site and has realised he cannot compare it to the others cleanly.
If they push back

Restaurant365 already does this.

R365 is only as current as what has been entered into it, and it does not know what Toast rang up an hour ago. The decision you make on a Wednesday happens before R365 has the data to inform it.

My managers know their numbers.

Each on their own definition. Ask two of them how they calculate labour percentage — whether salaried managers are in it, whether it is against gross or net sales. You will get two answers, and both will be sincere.

We are seasonal, it swings around.

Then comparing your sites to each other matters more, not less. Seasonality hits all of them; the gap between them is the part you control.

Segment 05

Professional services

Where the margin leaks between recorded and collected

Systems: practice management · time-and-billing · the CRM. Numbers: utilization, realization rate, WIP, revenue per head.

What they cannot see today

When the billable hour is the product, the gap between recorded time and collected revenue is the whole margin — and it only becomes visible after billing, when it is already a write-off.

Realization by matter, by partner and by team is a quarterly exercise, if it happens at all.

What fast data lets them do

See a matter's realization slipping while the work is still open and have the conversation with the client then — rather than writing the time off at billing and calling it the cost of doing business.

Rebalance work across a team in the month it matters, not after the quarter.

What that is worth

One point of realization on a year's billings. They know what they billed last year; that is the whole calculation.

Then bring one partner's utilization to the firm average and hold it — and note that nobody had to work longer for either of those.

Who in your network is this
  • Bills by the hour or by the matter — law, accounting, engineering, agency, consulting.
  • Has more than one partner or team, so the averages have somewhere to hide.
  • Writes off time at billing and calls it normal. Everyone in this segment does; almost nobody knows the number.
If they push back

Practice management already reports utilization.

On recorded time. It does not know what was actually collected, and the difference between those two is the only number that matters. Utilization can be up and the firm can be poorer.

Our people are fully booked.

Booked, billable and collected are three different numbers. Being busy is not the same as being paid, and a fully booked team writing off fifteen per cent at billing is the most expensive kind of busy.

Every matter is different.

Which is exactly why an average hides it. The point is seeing which ones differ, while you can still do something about them.

Segment 06

PE portfolio companies

Where the same word means different things at each company

Systems: each company's own ERP, CRM and finance stack · the consolidation tool. Numbers: EBITDA, working capital, pipeline, value-creation milestones.

What they cannot see today

Every company arrives with its own stack, so rolling the portfolio up is a monthly scramble — and “EBITDA” quietly means something slightly different at each one.

Which means the comparison between companies, the thing the whole model rests on, is not quite real.

What fast data lets them do

See working capital moving at a company in week two of the month, not in a board pack six weeks later when the quarter is already written.

Compare companies on one definition and know the comparison is real — so a management conversation is about performance rather than about methodology.

What that is worth

A month of earlier warning on a covenant. Anyone who has been near one knows exactly what that month is worth.

Then the consolidation scramble itself: the days it takes, at every company, every month, for twelve months.

Who in your network is this
  • Bought a company in the last two years and cannot get clean numbers out of it. This is the single strongest tell in the whole document.
  • Holds more than two operating companies, however informally — it does not have to be a fund.
  • Has a CFO whose month-end is a scramble across entities, and who has stopped complaining about it.
If they push back

We get monthly reporting from each company.

In each company's own format, on each company's own definitions. The consolidation is a person with a spreadsheet — and that person is the system. Ask what happens the month they are away.

Our companies use different systems and always will.

That is the assumption this is built on. Nothing gets replaced. The systems stay where they are and the definitions get reconciled on top of them.

That is what we have a CFO for.

You do — and this is what he is spending three days a month on. The question is not whether he can do it. It is whether that is the highest-value thing he does all month.

If you remember one thing

Ask the question, then stop talking

“By the time you see those numbers, can you still do anything about them?”

Every owner has felt this exact frustration and almost nobody has ever named it back to them. It works in all six segments because it is not about software — it is about the gap between finding out and being able to act.

You do not need to know their systems, their metrics or their industry to ask it. If the answer is a pause, that is the referral.
