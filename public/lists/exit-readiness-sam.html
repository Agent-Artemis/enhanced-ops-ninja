Exit Readiness

For owners selling in the next one to three years · prepared for Coach Sam

Sam — I know five men in your organisation who are inside this window, and I haven't spoken to any of them. I wouldn't, without asking you first. It's your house. This page is so you can decide whether it's something you'd want offered to your men at all.

1What this is

I sit down with an owner before he goes to market, find what a buyer is going to find, and tell him what it will cost him — while there's still time to fix it.

2Who it's for

  • An owner who intends to sell in the next one to three years.
  • An owner who has already been approached and hasn't decided yet.

Not everyone. If he isn't selling and isn't being courted, this isn't for him and I'd say so. A man who has just started building has no use for this and I'm not going to pretend otherwise.

3Why deals die — and why nobody catches it

Deals die in week six, on facts the owner never disclosed.

Not on bad analysis. On things nobody was told — until a buyer's team finds them, and then it's leverage instead of a problem you were fixing.

And everyone already in the room has a structural reason he won't hear it:

The bankerIs selling the company. Admitting a problem feels like arguing against his own price.
The accountantIs a vendor the owner has managed for years. That relationship doesn't invite hard truths.
The lawyerArrives late — usually after the things that matter are already fixed or already missed.
The buyerIs the adversary. Anything he learns, he uses.
I arrive without that problem — not through any technique, but because I've spent years in rooms where men say the thing they're ashamed of. That's the room you built, Sam. I'm not selling his company and I'm not on his payroll, so there's nothing for him to manage when he tells me the truth.

4How it works — two phases

Phase 1 — The assessment I sit with him and get the things he hasn't told anyone. Then I read the numbers. What comes out is a ranked list: what a buyer will find, what each one will cost him, and which are still fixable. Some won't be, and he needs to know that too.
Phase 2 — Keeping it fixed An ongoing retainer that keeps those items fixed and the numbers defensible all the way through to the sale. This is where the real value sits — a list of problems he doesn't act on is worth nothing. Phase 2 is the business intelligence work I already do. Not a second product — the same work, with a deadline attached and a reason to exist that it never had on its own.

5What I'd be asking of you

An introduction. Nothing more than that.

You're not selling anything, you're not recommending it, and you're not vouching for how it turns out. If a man says no, that's the end of it and it doesn't come back to you. I'd rather have your blessing and five conversations than go around you and have fifty.

And if you'd rather I didn't offer this to your men at all, say so on Friday and I'll leave it there.

Two things I'm not going to claim

This isn't a new category. Exit-readiness advisory exists and there are firms doing it — Umbrex, PCE, Roadmap Advisors, Exit Ready Advisors among them. What's different here is the method, not the idea: the assessment starts with what he hasn't said, and phase 2 actually keeps it fixed.

And I haven't done one yet. This business has run zero engagements. I'm not going to show you results I don't have. What I have is the work I already do on the numbers, and a reason it matters more when there's a sale coming.
