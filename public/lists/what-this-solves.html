Sam — these are sentences you have heard said out loud in your rooms. Every one of them has a price at the table, and the man saying it almost never knows what that price is. Read them and see whose face turns up.

The sentences

What owners say — and what it costs them

“The business can’t run without me.”
What it costs

A buyer who needs the owner in the building is buying a job, not a business — and he prices a job. It usually comes back as an earn-out instead of cash: the owner stays on, and only gets paid if the business performs without the very thing that makes it perform.

“I want out in a couple of years, but I don’t know what it’s worth.”
What it costs

Without a number he trusts, every offer sounds either insulting or generous and he cannot tell which. Men accept low offers and walk away from fair ones for the same reason — they had nothing to measure it against.

“One customer is a third of my revenue.”
What it costs

Concentration is risk the buyer would be inheriting, so he does not pay for it up front. It rarely kills the deal — it moves the money, into a holdback or an earn-out tied to that one customer still being there in two years.

“I don’t really have a management team under me.”
What it costs

The buyer is not only buying the owner out, he is deciding who runs it on the Monday after closing. If the answer is nobody, he brings his own people and pays less for what he is replacing — or he passes, and never says that was the reason.

“My books are a mess.” — or an accountant who produces whatever the owner tells him to produce.
What it costs

Numbers that cannot be tied out do not just look bad, they stop the clock. Diligence stretches, the deal ages, and every extra week is another week for something else to go wrong. Figures from an accountant who reports to the seller get rebuilt from scratch by the buyer’s team — and they rebuild them unkindly.

“I’ve been running personal things through the business.”
What it costs

Add-backs are ordinary and defensible — until they are found rather than disclosed. Found means the buyer stops trusting the rest of the file, re-checks everything, and re-opens the price on all of it.

“My partner and I don’t agree about selling.”
What it costs

A buyer will not spend six months and real money on a deal that two owners cannot both sign. Most walk at the first sign of it, early and quietly, and the owners are left thinking the price was the problem.

“I got approached once and nothing came of it.”
What it costs

An approach that went nowhere usually went nowhere for a reason the owner was never told. Nobody sends a letter explaining why they lost interest. Whatever it was is still sitting there, waiting for the next buyer to find it.

Why it is not caught first

Nobody in the room can tell him

Deals die in week six, on facts the owner never disclosed.

Not on bad analysis. On things nobody was told — until a buyer’s team finds them, and then it is leverage instead of a problem that could have been fixed.

The bankerIs selling the company. Admitting a problem feels like arguing against his own price.
The accountantIs a vendor the owner has managed for years. That relationship does not invite hard truths.
The buyerIs the adversary. Anything he learns, he uses.
I have not spoken to any of your men, and I would not without asking you first. It is your house.
What we actually do about it

How it gets fixed

Every sentence above is something a buyer will find on his own timetable. The work is finding it first, on ours — and there are two shapes to it, because they are two different jobs.

One-time · per deal
Get it ready

I sit with the owner and get the things he has not told anyone. Then I read the numbers. What comes out is a ranked list of what a buyer will find, what each one will cost him, and which are still fixable — then we fix the ones that are.

  • The books tied out, so the figures survive somebody else’s arithmetic.
  • Add-backs written down and defensible before they are discovered.
  • Concentration, key-man and management gaps named early, while there is still time to act.
  • He walks in knowing what his own file says.

This one ends. It is a bounded piece of work with a finish line, and most owners need it.

Ongoing · while he is a client
Keep it that way

A file that was clean once does not stay clean. Systems drift, someone adds a tool, a new spreadsheet appears. The Ninja keeps the systems connected and the numbers in one agreed set, so what we straightened does not quietly go back.

  • One set of numbers everybody in the business is working from.
  • It stays true between now and a sale that might be two years out.
  • He is not rebuilding the file the week a buyer asks for it.

This one continues for as long as he is a client, and stops when he stops.

Most men need the first. Only some want the second. If a man is eighteen months out and his numbers are a mess, he needs the readiness work and that is the whole conversation. The ongoing piece is for the ones who have decided, and who do not want to do this twice.

If a sentence up there put a face in your head, that is the whole point of this page. The problems are fixable while a man still has time. They are not fixable in week six.
