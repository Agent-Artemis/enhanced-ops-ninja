1Who this is for

You are not looking for a company type. You are listening for one of these sentences.

  • Our numbers never match.Two systems, two answers, nobody knows which is right.
  • I find out at month end.By the time they see it, they can't do anything about it.
  • My controller spends three days on the board pack.A good person doing the most expensive version of the job.
  • We're about to sell and diligence is a mess.Clean numbers lift the multiple. Fragmented ones compress it.
  • It all comes down to one spreadsheet.Ask who owns it — and what happens when they're on holiday.
The size test, if you need one: more than one location, and more than two systems that don't talk to each other. It isn't about revenue — it's about how many places a number can hide.

3What to say — and what not to

⛔ NEVER QUOTE A NUMBER

Not a figure, not a range, not “around”. The number depends on how many systems and locations connect, and Jeff frames it on a call. A number met cold is a number argued with. You can still answer the question — just not with a number.

✓ IF THEY ASK WHAT IT COSTS, SAY THIS
“Depending on the complexity, it costs the equivalent of a low to mid-level employee.” That is the answer. It gives them a real frame and contains no number, so there is nothing to argue with. Then hand off:
“Jeff will walk you through what it looks like for you — and he'll look at your setup first and tell you what he'd do, free.” Answer, then hand off. You've given them something and moved them to the audit in two sentences.
✓ YOUR JOB IS THE INTRODUCTION, NOT THE SALE

You don't have to explain how it works. If they've said one of the five sentences above, you've already qualified them. Get the intro and stop.

4What happens after you introduce someone

Jeff runs a free visibility audit. No cost, no software to install, no commitment.

He looks at what systems they run, where the numbers disagree, and tells them what he'd fix first — whether or not they ever buy anything.

This is why the introduction is easy to make. You're not spending your credibility on a sales call. You're giving your contact something useful and letting them decide.

On the call Jeff asks about ten questions — how they find out how last month went, what systems they run, and what number they most wish they could see every morning but can't. Nothing to prepare.

The introduction worth most

Listen for anyone thinking about selling in the next couple of years — or anyone whose advisor has told them to tidy the books. That is the single most valuable introduction you can make, and here is why.

  • Clean numbers raise what a buyer will payA buyer discounts what they cannot verify. If the numbers can't be stood up, they price that uncertainty in — and the seller pays for it.
  • They speed the sale upEvery question that can be answered from the system is a question nobody has to go and reconstruct.
  • Less due diligenceMessy books stretch diligence out, and anything that surfaces late gives the buyer a reason to come back and lower the price.
You are not selling them reporting. You are selling the value of their exit — and that is a conversation an owner will always take.

What they'll say back

The seven you'll hear most. You don't need to win these — you need to not get stuck.

“I already have a dashboard.”Does everyone in the room trust the same number? Most dashboards get quietly checked against somebody's own spreadsheet before anyone acts on them.
“My controller pulls the numbers.”She does, and she's good at it — that's the problem. How many days a month, and what would you rather she did with them?
“My data is a mess.”That's the job, not a prerequisite. You don't tidy the house before the cleaner arrives.
“We're too small for this.”More than one location and more than two systems that don't talk? That's exactly the size. It's not about revenue.
“How long does it take?”Two to three weeks to the first live number. Not a pilot — a number on a screen you can use.
“What if we stop?”Your data is yours and never leaves your own accounts — we join as an invited guest and you can remove us. Stop and you keep every system and every number you had.
“It's expensive.”Don't defend it and don't price it. “Against what?” — then hand to Jeff. He turns it into a comparison on the call.

If you only remember one line

“Your numbers are in six systems and none of them agree. He makes them agree — before your 7am huddle.”

And if they're thinking about selling: clean numbers lift the multiple. That one changes the whole conversation.
